Mastercard. The Story of a Corporate Disillusionment
July 2026

Lately, I have written a great deal about Africa.

Why?

First, because I own a stake in a real mineral deposit there. Not a project that exists only on paper, not another polished presentation for investors, but an actual mining asset that I have been developing for many years. Second, because that is where many of my closest friends live. Solomon, Shandy, and many others whom I have known for nearly thirty years. Over that time, they long ago ceased to be merely business partners.

Writing about payments and money transfers today has become much more difficult. Almost any subject quickly leads either into the high-risk industry—casinos, gambling, sports betting, and other complicated sectors—or into sanctions, restrictions, and the various mechanisms used to organize international settlements. That is an entirely different territory, where even a careless phrase can easily be interpreted in a way that was never intended.

Yet it was the payments industry that largely shaped my professional life.

Unistream introduced me to the world of money transfers. Quite by accident, in fact, at a time when the collapse of my own business had left me with almost nothing, forcing me to start over from scratch.

ChronoPay opened the door to internet acquiring, e-commerce, and global online payments.

And Mastercard introduced me to an entirely different world—the world of Western corporate culture.

That is the story I want to tell.

It is a story about admiration, hope, and what I once perceived as betrayal. Only years later did I realize that what had happened was not betrayal in the ordinary human sense. It was simply business as usual—impartial, cold, and completely emotionless capitalism.

Perhaps it is precisely this model that has fueled such strong demand in the West, particularly in the United States, for radical left-wing ideas. The truth is that political extremes of any kind often do just as much harm as even the noblest intentions can do good.

At the time, I was working for a major international company—one of the global leaders in payment technology. I was responsible for business development across Eastern Europe.

Then Mastercard acquired our company.

The very prospect of joining Mastercard was both exciting and inspiring. For anyone working in the payments industry, it was a truly significant event. It seemed to me that entirely new opportunities were about to unfold.

Almost immediately after our team was integrated into the Mastercard ecosystem, I began to realize that the company was building something far greater than simply an international card network. This became especially evident after Visa acquired Earthport. It was clear that both payment networks were gradually transforming themselves into global infrastructures for cross-border payments, aiming to become something comparable to SWIFT.

This was an entirely different level of business.

We were integrated into Mastercard's internal communities.

We gained access to the corporate portal, internal seminars, professional discussions, and an enormous network of employees around the world.

What impressed me most was the company's atmosphere.

Today, people would call it inclusivity.

At the time, however, it felt like something much deeper—a genuine corporate philosophy.

Everything revolved around people.

Around respect.

Around diversity.

Around the belief that the company existed not only to generate profits but also to improve people's lives.

There was constant talk about values, responsibility, teamwork, and mutual support.

I communicated directly with exceptionally capable lawyers, compliance executives from New York, and specialists from numerous departments. We were constantly reminded of how important every employee was and how significant the company's mission had become.

At times, it felt less like working for a commercial corporation and more like serving inside a state the size of a small European country.

Then everything changed.

First, our compliance team in London suddenly disappeared.

Simply disappeared.

Then an HR manager from Mastercard's Moscow office arrived.

Until then, the Moscow office had played almost no role in our work. During the previous year, we had barely interacted at all, apart from a single meeting with a Mastercard representative who, if I remember correctly, had come from Brussels.

Now all of us were gathered together.

After the general meeting, the HR manager announced that she wanted to speak with each employee individually.

I have no idea what she discussed with my colleagues.

What she told me was that the company highly valued my experience and wanted to discuss my future career directly within Mastercard. For that purpose, I was invited to the Moscow office for a meeting with one of the company's international executives.

Then she asked me one more question.

Would I object if my current employment contract were terminated in exchange for a severance package?

To me, the proposal seemed entirely natural.

After everything I had seen inside Mastercard, I had absolutely no reason to doubt the sincerity of what she was saying.

I agreed.

Our employment contracts were terminated.

Everyone received severance compensation.

Almost immediately afterward, I was invited to Mastercard's Moscow headquarters on Tsvetnoy Boulevard.

I arrived as scheduled.

I was escorted into a meeting room, where a video conference had been arranged with a regional executive based in Turkey.

We had never worked together before.

In fact, throughout my years at the company that Mastercard had acquired, our professional paths had never crossed.

This was despite the fact that I had been involved in a wide range of international projects, including obtaining money transfer licenses in multiple jurisdictions.

The conversation was brief.

Within only a few minutes, it became obvious that there would be no discussion about my future career.

There was no position waiting for me.

The opportunity that had supposedly justified inviting me to Mastercard's Moscow office simply did not exist.

For the first time, I realized that the entire process had most likely been nothing more than part of a carefully designed corporate procedure—a way to complete employee terminations smoothly, without conflict or unnecessary questions.

At that moment, the entire narrative about inclusivity, shared values, corporate family, and common purpose collapsed completely for me.

And it happened during one of the most difficult periods imaginable—the COVID-19 pandemic.

Our entire team, our entire business unit, was simply removed from the company's structure and sent on its way with three months' salary, exactly as happens during an ordinary workforce reduction.

At the time, I took the experience very personally.

I was genuinely disappointed not only with the company itself but, to some extent, with the entire Western corporate model that I had come to idealize.

Eventually, the emotions faded.

But one important lesson remained.

A large multinational corporation is not a family.

It never was.

It has no obligation to love its employees.

Its responsibility is to manage capital efficiently, fulfill its obligations to shareholders, and make decisions based solely on the interests of the business.

Everything else—corporate culture, inspiring talk about values, stories about a shared mission, the feeling of belonging to something larger and more meaningful—exists only for as long as it serves the company's interests.

When those interests change, the people change as well.

In the end, only the contract remains.

That was the moment I truly understood the meaning of the expression business as usual.

Nothing personal.

Just business.
