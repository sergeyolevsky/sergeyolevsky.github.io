Democrats: How the Party of the People Became the Party of the Elites
August 2026

From the working class to the knowing class: how education, professional culture and identity politics changed the social face of the Democratic Party

Once upon a time, the Democratic Party was the party of the working class.

Labor unions, factory workers, the urban poor, immigrants, people without a college education — this social base formed a significant part of the Democratic coalition for decades.

American politics looks very different today.

The more education an American voter has, the more likely they are to vote Democratic. In 2024, voters with a four-year college degree supported Kamala Harris by a margin of 16 percentage points, while voters without a college degree supported Donald Trump by a margin of 14 points. Among Americans with postgraduate degrees, the gap was even larger: 65% to 33% in favor of Harris.

But something more happened than a simple “working-class exodus” from the Democrats.

A new social group moved into the Democratic coalition.

Highly educated professionals.

Academics. Lawyers. Journalists. Employees of government and nonprofit organizations. Corporate management. Big Tech. Hollywood. Consultants. HR professionals.

This group increasingly determines not only the electoral face of the party, but also its language.

And along with this new social class came a new ideology.

Woke

Woke ideology is often reduced to pronouns, gender, or endless arguments about which word is considered acceptable today.

That is a superficial view.

At its core, Woke ideology rests on a much more serious framework: society is viewed primarily through the lens of identity, group membership and power relations between groups.

Who is privileged?

Who is marginalized?

Who is oppressed?

Who has the right to speak?

Whose experience constitutes lived experience and therefore deserves a special kind of authority?

Which words merely describe reality, and which ones, on the contrary, reproduce structures of power?

From this come:

• systemic racism;

• privilege;

• intersectionality;

• microaggressions;

• unconscious bias;

• gender identity;

• cultural appropriation;

• personal lived experience as a source of special knowledge;

• Diversity, Equity and Inclusion — DEI.

Some of these concepts can be perfectly useful analytical tools.

The problem begins when they are turned into a universal theory of society.

Because along with this theory comes a new kind of elite.

Not necessarily the richest.

Not necessarily the most powerful.

But the people who know how to correctly interpret what is happening.

An ordinary person sees one person getting a job while another does not.

A social-justice specialist may see an expression of structural advantage.

An ordinary person hears an unfortunate joke.

A specialist identifies a microaggression.

An ordinary person sees a difference in outcomes between groups.

A specialist begins looking for the system of power that produced that difference.

And gradually a new kind of authority emerges:

I see what you do not see.

If you do not see the problem, that does not prove that the problem does not exist.

Perhaps you simply have not been sufficiently trained to see it.

This is where Woke becomes an ideology of interpreters.

The New Elite

The old aristocracy had a simple basis for superiority:

We were born into the right family.

Technocracy had its own:

We have the data.

Bureaucracy had another:

We know how the state works.

The new cultural elite says:

We understand structures that you are unable to see.

This is an exceptionally convenient position.

Because it allows a person to be inside the elite while simultaneously considering themselves a fighter against the elite.

You can have a prestigious education, a high salary, live in an expensive liberal metropolis, work at a university, corporation or media organization — and at the same time consider yourself someone fighting against privilege.

It produces a wonderful construction:

I belong to the elite, but because I am aware of my privilege, my position within the elite ceases to be a problem.

This is no longer classical politics.

It is a moral and cultural hierarchy.

Mastercard and “professional” identity

And here I want to give an example not from American politics, but from my own professional experience.

In my previous work at Mastercard, I encountered a person who held a very senior position at the company — Executive Vice President and Chief Inclusion Officer.

This was not an invented position. Mastercard did indeed appoint Randall M. Tucker as Chief Inclusion Officer and, after his promotion, as Executive Vice President. His work was directly connected with the development of Diversity, Equity and Inclusion.

But there was another detail that always amused me.

He has a LinkedIn profile.

Normally, LinkedIn is a rather strange, but nevertheless professional, space. People write there:

payment executive;

technology strategist;

banking specialist;

product leader;

former CEO;

20 years in financial services.

In other words, they describe what they know how to do.

But Randall Tucker's public profile description says:

“Black. Gay. Strategist. Builder. Leader. Global.”

And this is much more interesting to me than the person himself or his sexual orientation.

Because the first two words answer the question:

Who are you?

And the next four:

What can you do?

And that order is extremely revealing.

We have arrived at a culture in which identity becomes a professional characteristic.

Not:

“I know how to build global teams.”

But:

“I am Black. I am Gay. And only then am I a Strategist and Builder.”

This is a very characteristic product of the new corporate culture.

And here an important qualification is necessary.

There is nothing wrong with having a different skin color.

There is nothing wrong with belonging to a sexual minority.

And there is nothing wrong with being proud of your identity.

The question is completely different:

Why do these characteristics become part of the professional brand of a person working in business?

If I hire someone to run an international payments business, what matters to me first is:

Can they build a business?

Can they manage people?

Do they understand the market?

Can they make decisions?

Do they understand the product?

Can they take responsibility for results?

Their skin color and sexual orientation tell me none of these things by themselves.

But the new DEI culture has gradually made identity itself part of professional capital.

And that is a fundamentally different system.

When Diversity becomes a profession

The next step was practically inevitable.

If society is viewed through the lens of identity, then organizations need specialists who will manage that identity within them.

This is how we get:

• Chief Diversity Officers;

• DEI departments;

• Diversity consultants;

• Inclusive Leadership training;

• Employee Resource Groups;

• Bias training;

• Equity assessments;

• Inclusive language policies.

And all of this begins to develop a life of its own.

At Mastercard, for example, DEI became a separate strategic function. The company set a specific goal of increasing the representation of Black leaders at the VP level and above by 50% by 2025. In 2023, Mastercard reported that the number of Black leaders at VP level and above had increased from 72 to 80, with cumulative growth since 2020 reaching 13%.

And here a perfectly legitimate question arises:

If Diversity becomes a separate management function, with its own executives, budgets, KPIs and career paths, does the fight against discrimination itself become a new form of corporate bureaucracy?

The paradox is that the fight against social hierarchies itself begins to create a hierarchy.

A person appears whose professional role is to explain to other people how correctly they should relate to identity.

And that person receives a position.

A salary.

A team.

A budget.

And a career.

Thus the fight against the system gradually becomes part of the system.

Language as a social passport

Woke culture is particularly interesting because it has turned language into one of the main markers of belonging.

You know the right words — you are inside.

You do not know them — someone will explain them to you.

You use the wrong word — perhaps the problem is already with you.

A person says:

“Why can't we simply say man or woman?”

The answer is:

“You do not understand the complexity of gender identity.”

They ask:

“What is intersectionality?”

They are given an explanation of intersectionality.

They ask:

“And why do I need to know this?”

And this is where the real problem emerges.

Because the answer effectively becomes:

Because an educated person is supposed to know this.

Politics has therefore turned into an examination of cultural belonging.

Kwanzaa and American Africa

Kwanzaa is a wonderful example.

The holiday was created in the United States in 1966 by Maulana Karenga as an African-American cultural celebration. The name itself is associated with the Swahili phrase matunda ya kwanza — “first fruits.”

So this is not an ancient African tradition that somehow arrived in America.

It is a modern American cultural construction, consciously created in the context of Black Power.

And this is where a small linguistic comedy begins.

In Hausa — one of the major languages of West Africa — there is a word banza, meaning “useless,” “worthless,” or “of no value.” In Hausa dictionaries it is explicitly defined as “useless” and “worthless,” while the expression a banza means “in vain” or “without benefit.”

Of course, there is no etymological connection between Kwanzaa and the Hausa word banza.

But the resemblance is magnificent.

And one cannot help asking:

Did the people who invented this holiday actually have a very deep understanding of Africa?

Because Africa is not one culture.

Not one language.

Not one history.

And certainly not a single cultural package that can be assembled at an American university from a few symbols, seven principles and a couple of Swahili words.

If the creators had actually studied West African languages in depth, perhaps someone would have noticed this little linguistic detail.

But of course, this is just a joke.

And that is precisely why it works.

Because behind it lies a perfectly serious problem:

Africa in American cultural discourse sometimes becomes not real Africa, but an Africa constructed by the American cultural elite.

The Pentagon starts to look like HR

Now let us move this framework into a place where it looks particularly strange.

The Pentagon.

Under the Biden administration, DEI and questions of gender identity did indeed become part of official government policy. The administration restored the ability of transgender people to serve in the armed forces in accordance with their gender identity, while the broader issue of Diversity became a visible part of the military establishment's public agenda.

But what is particularly revealing is not simply the existence of such policies, but the penetration of this kind of logic into military and intelligence institutions.

In 2024, a DEI-related material from the U.S. intelligence community became public that, among other things, discussed cross-dressing and inclusive language. In the material, an intelligence employee described their own experience of wearing women's clothing and connected it with the development of empathy and professional qualities. The material was distributed through the Diversity, Equity, Inclusion and Accessibility Office within the Office of the Director of National Intelligence.

And here I want to ask a very practical question:

Who exactly were they trying to impress with this demonstration of progressivism?

Iran?

The Chinese military?

North Korea?

I can imagine the closed-door briefing:

— We have a serious problem.

— The Chinese navy?

— No.

— North Korean missiles?

— No.

— Iran?

— No.

— Then what?

— Our language is not inclusive enough.

Of course, this is a caricature.

But that is precisely why it works so well.

The Pentagon is not a university campus and not corporate HR.

Its mission is much simpler — and obvious to everyone.

And therefore an uncomfortable question arises:

When a government organization begins to demonstrate primarily its own cultural progressivism, who is that signal actually aimed at — an external adversary or its own cultural elite?

Democrats and the cult of education

And here we return to the Democratic Party itself.

Its social base has changed.

In 1996, around 22% of Democrats had a bachelor's degree or higher. By 2023, the figure had risen to around 45%. At the same time, the share of Democrats with no college experience at all fell from 51% to 25%.

This does not mean that educated people are obliged to be Democrats.

And it does not mean that there are no workers among Democrats.

But it does mean that the educated professional class has become a much more important part of the Democratic coalition.

And with it came a new way of looking at society.

A person with a degree now possesses not only a professional advantage.

Increasingly, they also receive moral and political authority.

If you have a college education, you are presumed to understand the world better.

If you are educated and Woke, you understand hidden structures of power as well.

And if you do not understand?

Well.

You need to be enlightened.

The party that explains people's own lives to them

And here we encounter the central paradox.

An old-school Democrat might have told a worker:

We want to increase your wages.

The modern cultural Democrat often begins somewhere else:

Your identity exists within a system of power relations.

Your language may reproduce structural inequality.

Your experience is not necessarily universal.

Your privilege may be invisible to you yourself.

You must take into account the lived experiences of other groups.

Only after that can we move on to politics.

But the person may have a much simpler list of problems:

the price of groceries.

Rent.

A mortgage.

Health insurance.

Wages.

A job.

And when they are offered yet another seminar on intersectionality instead of an answer, it is perfectly natural for them to start asking:

Guys, do you even remember why the party exists?

From the working class to the knowing class

Perhaps this is where the most important transformation took place.

The Democrats did not simply become the party of more educated people.

They gradually became the party of people who regard education and cultural competence as the basis of political authority.

In other words, the new elite does not say:

“I have more money.”

And not even:

“I have more power.”

It says:

“I understand what is happening better.”

This is perhaps the hardest form of elitism to recognize.

Because a person can sincerely consider themselves a fighter against privilege.

They simply do not notice that their most important privileged resource has become the right to explain to other people how the world works.

The irony of history

For decades, the Democratic Party helped people obtain education and climb the social ladder.

But when a significant portion of those people climbed upward, they brought their own culture with them.

And gradually a nearly classical transformation took place:

The party defended the interests of people who wanted to enter the elite;

those people entered the elite;

and then the party began defending the worldview of the elite itself.

And now an uncomfortable question arises:

Are Democrats still speaking on behalf of the working class — or are they telling the working class how it should understand its own life?

These are two completely different things.

Afterword

The biggest problem facing the modern Democratic Party is not that it has become too left-wing.

And not even that it has become too Woke.

The problem is deeper.

It has become the party of a social group that increasingly treats its own cultural framework as universal.

And this produces a strange phenomenon.

The farther an ordinary American is from universities, Big Tech, the media, NGOs, corporate HR and cultural institutions, the more they are expected to be told why they should accept the language and categories of those institutions.

And if they do not accept them?

They are told that they are not educated enough.

Not sensitive enough.

Not enlightened enough.

They do not understand the structure well enough.

And this is where the party that once said:

“We will give you a voice,”

turns into a party that says:

“We will explain to you what your voice actually means.”

And then it becomes clear why American politics is so polarized today.

One part of the country says:

“Don't tell me how to live.”

And the other responds:

“No, you simply do not yet understand well enough how the world works.”

And somewhere between them stands the very person for whom the Democratic Party once existed.

He simply wants to know:

Why do his groceries, insurance and housing cost so much — and why does his political party now answer him with a lecture about his privilege?
