Africa, the Slave Trade and the History People Prefer Not to Remember
September 2026

The modern political picture of history often looks extremely simple: Europeans were the oppressors, Africans were exclusively victims, and the entire history of the slave trade can be reduced to white Europeans arriving in Africa and beginning to take people away.

There is only one problem: history was considerably more complicated.

Slavery and the trafficking of people existed in Africa long before Europeans appeared on the western coast of the continent. Different regions had their own forms of dependency, slavery, military raids and human trafficking, while the scale and character of these practices varied greatly from one society to another.

And the most uncomfortable fact for the modern simplified picture is that a significant proportion of the people who ended up on European ships reached them through African political, military and trading structures.

Africans Sold Africans

A very simple question arises: if Europeans themselves did not capture most of the people deep inside the continent, where did they obtain so many slaves?

The answer lies in the structure of the trade itself.

In different regions of West and Central Africa there were rulers, merchants, military formations and intermediaries who captured people during wars and raids, purchased them from other African groups, transported them to the coast and sold them to European and American traders.

Among the historically documented participants in these processes were, for example, the Vili, Efik, Dahomey, Aro and Imbangala.

This does not mean that there was some single “African slave-trading system.” Africa was never one state or one society. There were hundreds of political entities, peoples, cities and trading networks that constantly fought, formed alliances, traded and competed with one another.

But that is precisely why the statement “Africans were sold by Europeans” is historically incomplete without further explanation.

In many cases, a European trader purchased a person not from a European state, but from an African ruler, military leader or merchant.

First, a person was captured or placed into a dependent status within Africa.

They could then be sold to another African intermediary.

They would then reach the coast.

Only after that would they become part of the transatlantic trade.

According to estimates from SlaveVoyages, approximately 12.5 million Africans were embarked on ships as part of the transatlantic slave trade, while about 10.7 million survived the Middle Passage and were disembarked in the Americas.

Behind these figures stood a vast commercial system involving European carriers and buyers, American markets, and African political and trading structures that supplied people.

Efunroye Tinubu

And this is where one of the most interesting figures in the history of Lagos appears — Efunroye Tinubu.

She was a wealthy and influential nineteenth-century woman, trader and political figure who operated in Lagos during a period when the slave trade remained an important part of the regional economy.

Her biography is particularly inconvenient for the modern simplified picture.

She was African.

She was a woman.

She was an influential entrepreneur.

She possessed considerable wealth and political influence.

And she was also associated with the slave trade and slave ownership.

In other words, this was not a European colonizer who had arrived from London or Liverpool.

This was a member of the local African elite operating within her own African political and trading system and conducting business that included involvement in the slave trade.

Biographies like this demonstrate particularly well how poorly the simple “Europeans versus Africans” framework explains history.

When the British Decided to Close the Market

At the beginning of the nineteenth century, Britain moved from participating in the transatlantic slave trade to actively suppressing it.

After the abolition of the slave trade, the British Navy began intercepting ships carrying slaves, while British authorities concluded agreements with African rulers and gradually increased pressure on coastal trading centers.

Lagos became one of those centers.

In 1851, the British intervened in the situation in Lagos, and the subsequent development of relations gradually led to the abandonment of the previous economic model, which was based to a significant extent on the slave trade.

For some members of the local elite, this was a serious economic problem.

Because the slave trade generated money.

And when the British began closing that market, resistance was entirely understandable from the perspective of the economic interests of those who profited from it.

Gradually, the trade in people was replaced by what became known as legitimate commerce — trade in commodities, above all palm oil and other agricultural products.

So this was not simply a change in legislation.

It was the restructuring of an entire economic system.

This Does Not Make Europeans Innocent — But It Changes the Picture

Europeans undoubtedly participated in the slave trade and gained enormous economic benefits from it.

Brazil was one of the largest destinations for enslaved people.

European ships transported people across the Atlantic.

European trading companies and states profited from the system.

But this does not mean that African participation in the system can simply be erased from history.

Without African suppliers of people, the transatlantic system could not have existed on the scale that it did.

And here it is important to distinguish between two things.

The fact that European buyers created enormous demand for slaves does not mean that African societies were passive observers.

And the fact that African rulers and traders sold people to Europeans does not mean that Europeans did not bear responsibility for their own role in the system.

Both sides participated in the same enormous commercial mechanism, although their roles, capabilities and benefits were different.

An Africa That Never Existed

Perhaps one of the biggest mistakes in the modern perception of Africa is that it is often presented as a single historical space.

But there was never one Africa.

There were states, empires, cities, tribes, trading alliances and military organizations.

Some fought one another.

Others traded.

Some captured prisoners.

Others sold them to their neighbors.

Some resisted the slave trade.

Others themselves became participants in it.

That is precisely why it is impossible simply to say “Africans did this” or “Africans did that.”

The next question must always be: which Africans, where, when, in which state and under what circumstances?

The history of the transatlantic slave trade is not a history of two monolithic sides.

It is a history of an enormous number of participants, interests and conflicts.

And in this history, Africans were not only victims.

They were also rulers, merchants, warriors, intermediaries, slave owners and political players.

And Now — the Surname

And now another detail becomes interesting.

Today, one of Nigeria's most recognizable political surnames belongs to the country's president — Bola Ahmed Tinubu.

No direct proven blood relationship between him and Efunroye Tinubu can be established.

A shared surname proves nothing by itself.

But the history of the Tinubu surname and its presence in Nigerian political and economic history are interesting enough to raise the question.

A direct connection?

Not proven.

A coincidence?

Certainly possible.

Beyond that lies the territory of family history, which cannot be established from a surname alone.

Still, who knows.

A Postscript from Nigeria

And one small personal episode that may be useful to remember for those who try to explain Africa exclusively through American or European ideas about race, class and social relations.

When I worked at a mine in Nigeria, ordinary workers addressed me as Master Serge.

Household staff usually simply said Master.

And this had nothing to do with the fact that I was white.

The household staff of wealthy Nigerians addressed their own employers in exactly the same way.

For someone who grew up in the United States or Europe, such a form of address may seem completely exotic or even impossible, because it does not fit the familiar Western system of ideas about equality, class and social relations.

But Nigeria is not America.

It has its own history, its own social hierarchies, its own traditions of address, and its own ideas about status, respect, age, wealth and a person's position.

And this is another good example of why Africa cannot be understood simply by transferring ready-made American or European political categories onto it.

Africa is not a political image invented solely to confirm a preconceived worldview.

It is a vast continent with its own history, in which there were states and empires, wars and trade, slavery and resistance, wealth and poverty, local elites and ordinary people, Europeans and Africans who cooperated with one another, fought one another and, yes, sold people to one another.

And if we genuinely want to understand this history, we have to begin precisely by acknowledging its complexity.
