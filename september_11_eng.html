September 11 — A Scar on the Face of the City
September 2026

September 11 is not simply a tragic day that changed New York and, perhaps, the entire world. For me, it is also a deeply personal story.

I learned about the tragedy of September 11 in Nigeria, in Jos.

International communications there were far from reliable at the time. Our satellite phone was unavailable, and above all I wanted to call New York and find out whether my family was all right.

I went to the home of Atiya Nasredin, the head of the NASCO Group. He was not there on the compound, however, and I did not want to wait. My friend Osman, Atiya's cousin, suggested that we try the neighboring house. Another man from Eritrea lived there — like Atiya and Osman themselves. I no longer remember his name. At the time, he was the CEO of Lion Bank in Nigeria and lived in Jos, a city that was then known for its pleasant climate and relatively peaceful life by Nigerian standards.

The owner was at home. Without asking any unnecessary questions, he let us in and immediately allowed us to use his satellite phone.

At that time, many people in Jos relied on satellite communications. Cellular service there, as I recall, was only beginning to appear, and perhaps it did not exist at all yet. In Lagos, mobile service was already operating — I remember it from 1997, when I first arrived in Nigeria.

I called New York.

My family was all right.

Osman and I thanked our host and went back.

At the time, we were renting a place in the NASCO Compound. The compound consisted of two sections located along one of the main roads in Jos, opposite NASCO offices and several of the company's businesses.

The name NASCO itself comes from the name of the company's founder and Atiya's father — Nasreddin Company.

But I had my own relationship with New York, and especially with the Twin Towers.

The World Trade Center had always had a special meaning for me. The offices of people I knew and friends of mine were located there. It was also where I first worked in an office.

Well, “worked” may be a little too strong.

In the early 1990s, I was essentially doing one of my first internships. I carried out various assignments for one of my father's friends and business partners, Yura Orlikov — or for one of his assistants.

At the time, I had, so to speak, two internship options: working with Yura or going to so-called Gold Street in Manhattan — a street where numerous jewelry companies were located. There was a wholesale company there owned by another acquaintance of my father, an Orthodox Jew, whose surname, I believe, was Sitrik.

Looking back today, I realize that I probably should have chosen the internship with Sitrik. I could have learned a great deal about the gold and diamond trade there.

I cannot say that I learned anything particularly useful from Yura. Except that it was there, in the early 1990s, that I first heard the names Deripaska and Chernoy. Yura was involved in metals, among other things.

But the feeling of the Twin Towers stayed with me.

It was cool there.

I remember helicopters flying so low that it seemed as though they were below the level of our office. Later, I returned to the Twin Towers many times and took friends there when they came from Moscow.

That is why I did not experience September 11 as a distant tragedy that had happened somewhere in America.

I experienced it as something personal.

It was as if a huge scar had suddenly appeared on the face of a city that was very important to me and to which so much of my life was connected.

Perhaps that is why it is almost impossible to explain my feelings about September 11 to someone who has never lived in New York and never had such a personal connection with the city.
