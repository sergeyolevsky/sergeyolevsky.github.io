When One Percent Destroys Trust.
A Few Questions for MTS and Those Who Design Customer Experience
July 2026

Today I came across a situation that, in my opinion, perfectly illustrates how a seemingly insignificant management decision can damage the overall perception of an entire company.

My home internet service from MTS is linked to my wife's mobile phone number. This morning the internet stopped working. Instead of access, I saw the following message:

"Your outstanding balance is 782 rubles. To restore the service, please pay the outstanding amount."

There was also a payment button.

I clicked it. The MTS payment page opened, and the amount was already filled in automatically — exactly 782 rubles and some kopecks. I entered my bank card details and paid precisely the amount that the system itself had suggested.

The internet did not come back.

At first I assumed the payment simply had not been processed yet. Since the bank card was not mine, I couldn't verify anything. Eventually I asked my relatives to top up the mobile account with another 800 rubles. Almost immediately afterward, the internet connection was restored.

Later that evening I discovered the real reason.

It turned out that when funding the account, MTS immediately deducts one percent of the payment amount as compensation for the payment processing fee. As a result, after paying exactly the amount that the company itself stated was sufficient to restore the service, a small outstanding balance still remained, and the service was not reactivated.

That immediately raised several questions.

First.

If the company knows that one percent will be deducted, why isn't that percentage included in the amount the system asks the customer to pay?

Why present an amount that is knowingly insufficient?

Second.

Why not restore the service once the stated outstanding balance has been paid and then charge the additional one percent separately afterward?

Third.

Why do the company's own acquiring costs suddenly become the customer's problem precisely at the moment when the customer faithfully follows every instruction provided by the system?

As someone who has spent many years working in international payments, acquiring, and payment product development, I can say one simple thing.

A good payment service is built around one very simple principle.

If the system tells the customer exactly how much to pay, then paying that amount should produce the expected result.

Everything else is part of the company's internal processes and should never be shifted onto the customer.

What makes this situation even more surprising is that it is not a technically difficult problem.

Adding one percent to an automatically calculated payment amount is literally a matter of writing just a few lines of code.

Which naturally leads to another question.

Has nobody inside the company ever gone through this payment process from the perspective of an ordinary customer?

Or are scenarios like this simply never tested?

Or did someone genuinely decide that a situation where a customer pays exactly what the system requests but still remains in debt represents an acceptable user experience?

If so, then the problem is far more serious than a one-percent fee.

I sincerely hope this was never a conscious decision approved by the company's senior management.

I would much rather believe that stories like this simply never reach the executives, and that mechanisms like this appear somewhere within individual departments without proper product oversight or customer experience review.

But if management is aware of this and considers such a model acceptable, then a much bigger question arises: how much does the company actually value customer trust today?

This is not about seven or eight rubles.

It is about principles.

It is about trust.

It is about management quality.

It is about attention to detail.

Because it is precisely these details that shape how customers perceive a brand.

Sometimes a company's reputation is damaged not by competitors or by crises.

Sometimes it is damaged by the person who once said, "Good enough."

And by the fact that nobody higher up ever asked, "How will the customer feel at that moment?"
