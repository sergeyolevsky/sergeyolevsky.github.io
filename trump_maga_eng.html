Trump and MAGA: Why a Man from the Elite Became the Symbol of Revolt Against the Elite
August 2026

There is a very simple way to write about Donald Trump.

You can make a list.

Lies. Tariffs. Failures. Scandals. Humiliations of allies. Conflicts with institutions. Attacks on the press. Attacks on scientists. Personnel chaos. Corruption scandals. Unpredictability.

The list could go on almost indefinitely.

But it seems to me that this approach no longer explains very much.

The problem with Trump today is not interesting because he has done many bad things. We already know that. The much more interesting question is this:

How did a man whose biography is so far removed from the social experience of a significant part of rural and working-class America become, for millions of people, a symbol of dignity, strength and “real America”?

And why is that connection proving to be so remarkably durable?

Recent polls show that Trump's nationwide support is indeed weakening. In one August Economist/YouGov poll, 33% of Americans approved of his job performance, while 62% disapproved. In a more recent poll, the figure recovered somewhat — to 36% versus 57%. But something much more interesting has happened inside the Republican coalition.

Among Republicans who identify as MAGA, roughly 95% approve of Trump. Among Republicans who do not consider themselves MAGA, the figure is around 59%.

This difference is much more important than Trump's overall approval rating.

Because it shows that MAGA is no longer simply a collection of political preferences.

It is a form of identity.

And this is where the really interesting story begins.

The strangest paradox of Trump

Imagine an American voter from the MAGA coalition.

He or she may live in Iowa, Oklahoma, West Virginia or rural Pennsylvania.

They may be a farmer.

An electrician.

A carpenter.

The owner of a small construction company.

A nurse.

A mechanic.

A truck driver.

An entrepreneur.

This needs to be emphasized immediately: MAGA cannot simply be reduced to incompetence or low education.

That would be too simple and, more importantly, intellectually lazy.

A person can have no university degree and still be an outstanding professional. A good electrician understands electricity better than most sociology professors. A good surgeon knows medicine better than almost any journalist. A good builder can spot a structural problem in five minutes that an engineer might spend an hour looking for.

So the question is not who is smarter.

The much more interesting question is:

Who has the right to be considered an authority outside their own professional field?

This is where the real dividing line appears.

Trump as the opposite of his own electorate

Trump's biography makes this question particularly paradoxical.

He is not a farmer.

Not a worker.

Not a veteran.

Not a man who rose from the very bottom.

Not a representative of rural America.

Donald Trump was born in New York and received from his family not simply money, but something much more important: capital, real estate, a name, connections and access to an existing business infrastructure.

His father, Fred Trump, built a substantial business in New York real estate. The company's history was connected not only with entrepreneurial ability, but also with government housing programs, political connections and allegations of discriminatory housing practices. In 1973, the federal government accused Trump Management of violating the Fair Housing Act; the case was ultimately resolved through a settlement.

Then came Donald himself.

He did not create capital from nothing.

He inherited it and turned his own name into a financial asset.

This is a fundamental distinction.

His main product gradually became not so much concrete, real estate or casinos, but Donald Trump himself.

A name.

A face.

A television character.

A tower.

A brand.

A promise of wealth.

In this sense, his career is remarkably consistent.

Trump sells not so much physical objects as the image of a person who possesses power.

That is why his political career looks less like a break with his previous life than its logical continuation.

Why the family history matters

Here an important clarification is necessary.

The history of Trump's ancestors is not an argument for inherited guilt.

Donald Trump is not responsible for the actions of his grandfather.

But the origins of family wealth are directly relevant to the question of the self-made myth.

Friedrich Trump, Donald's grandfather, did indeed begin his American career in the economy surrounding the gold rush and the services associated with it.

In Seattle, he acquired an establishment called the Poodle Dog in an area where, at the end of the nineteenth century, there was a developed industry involving alcohol, gambling and sex work. Advertising for the establishment did contain the phrase “Private Rooms for Ladies,” which biographer Gwenda Blair interpreted as a possible reference to prostitutes.

But it is important not to cross the boundary of what the evidence actually establishes. Blair herself later emphasized that she did not consider it proven that Friedrich Trump was a pimp or directly operated a brothel. Moreover, Seattle historians have challenged even an unequivocal interpretation of the advertising language.

This distinction matters.

We do not need to turn family history into a criminal case in order to see the structure.

The first generation makes money in the peripheral economy of the gold rush.

The second builds a huge business in New York real estate.

The third turns family capital into a global brand.

And then that third-generation representative becomes a symbol of the fight against the elites.

And this is where the real paradox begins.

Where did the myth of the man “outside the system” come from?

The most interesting thing about Trump is that he was never a man outside the system.

He was a man who became exceptionally good at using the system.

His early career in Manhattan was built on access to political connections, city decisions, tax incentives, real estate and people capable of opening doors.

His relationship with Roy Cohn is particularly revealing.

Cohn was one of New York's most famous political lawyers and a man with connections to figures in organized crime.

The construction of Trump Tower took place within New York's construction environment, where organized crime had deeply penetrated the concrete industry and construction unions. S&A Concrete, which supplied materials for one of Trump's projects, was connected to Anthony “Fat Tony” Salerno and Paul Castellano. Federal investigations and court documents extensively documented the role of these men and associated companies in the system controlling New York's construction business.

This does not mean that Trump was a member of the Mafia.

And this is precisely where caution is necessary.

Documented connections to a Mafia-influenced business environment are one thing.

Claiming that Trump himself was a member of an organized crime organization is something entirely different.

The first has a serious documentary basis.

The second does not.

But for our subject, even this is not the most important point.

What matters is that the man who would eventually become one of the most prominent political critics of the “corrupt elite” was himself formed inside one of America's most aggressive, closed and interconnected elite environments.

So why him?

This, in my view, is where the real psychology of MAGA begins.

Trump did not become one of them.

He offered his supporters something else.

He offered them the opportunity to stop thinking of themselves as losers.

And those are fundamentally different things.

Modern society constantly makes demands on people.

Study.

Understand things.

Improve your qualifications.

Read.

Check your sources.

Be able to explain your position.

Admit mistakes.

Change your mind when new evidence appears.

Accept that a doctor knows medicine better than you do.

That an engineer knows engineering better than you do.

That a scientist knows their field better than you do.

That a judge knows the law better than you do.

That a statistician understands statistics better than you do.

That a government specialist who has spent twenty years working on a particular problem probably knows more about it than a random person on Facebook.

For a mature society, this is a fairly natural arrangement.

But for someone who experiences this social hierarchy as humiliation, it can be unbearable.

Because it constantly reminds you:

There are fields in which another person objectively knows more than you.

And then there are two possible paths.

The first is to accept this as a normal part of adult life.

You can learn.

You can improve.

You can say: “I don't know.”

The second path is much easier.

You can declare the entire system of authority to be a fraud.

This is what Trump does

At least, this is the path Trump repeatedly offers his audience.

The professor becomes not someone who knows more, but an arrogant idiot.

The scientist becomes not an expert, but a grant recipient.

The journalist becomes not a professional, but an enemy.

The judge becomes not a holder of legal authority, but a political conspirator.

The government expert becomes not a specialist, but part of the deep state.

The university becomes not a place where knowledge is produced, but a machine for ideological indoctrination.

Expertise becomes not a way of reducing the probability of error, but a way of hiding the truth from the “ordinary person.”

And here a fundamental psychological transformation takes place.

If the expert cannot be trusted, I no longer need to become an expert in order to argue with him on equal terms.

If the scientist is a liar, I can ignore his scientific qualifications.

If the journalist is corrupt, I don't need to understand journalism.

If the economist is bought by corporations, I don't need to study economics.

If the university is ideologically captured, I don't need to read the research.

If the judge is corrupt, I don't need to know the law.

And suddenly a remarkably comfortable world emerges.

In that world, the individual no longer has to rise to the level of a complex system.

The system has to descend to his level.

This is not hatred of knowledge

And here I would once again challenge my own initial formulation.

This is not necessarily hatred of knowledge.

Nor is it necessarily the complex of an uneducated person.

There are enormous numbers of highly competent professionals among MAGA supporters.

Their problem — if the word “problem” is appropriate at all — may lie somewhere else.

It is a conflict between practical competence and institutional authority.

A person can be an excellent electrician and still reject the special authority of a climate scientist.

He can be a brilliant entrepreneur and distrust economists.

He can be an excellent physician and regard government bureaucracy as a gathering of idiots.

He can be a very intelligent person and still consider the university elite culturally and politically hostile.

So the question is not:

“Who is smarter — the plumber or the professor?”

That is a meaningless question.

The question is:

“Is a person willing to recognize that outside his own field there exists an epistemic asymmetry?”

In other words:

Am I willing to accept that another person genuinely knows something better than I do?

This is where the political problem begins.

Trump turns defeat into an identity

In this sense, Trump offers his supporter not a program, but a psychological bargain.

You didn't lose anything.

You were cheated.

You didn't fall behind.

You were humiliated.

You are not less competent.

You were simply kept out.

You don't have to become like them.

They have to stop being considered better than you.

This is a fundamental distinction.

Because there is one way to increase your own status:

Raise yourself.

And there is another:

Lower the status of someone else.

The first requires effort.

The second requires only an enemy.

And Trump has proved extraordinarily effective at the second.

He does not simply say: “I represent you.”

He says:

“I hate the same people you hate.”

And the more crudely he does it, the more authentic he appears.

His rudeness becomes evidence of honesty.

His ignorance becomes evidence that he is “not like them.”

His bad taste becomes evidence of independence from cultural rules.

His narcissism becomes a source of strength.

His constant conflicts with institutions become evidence that the institutions really are against him.

It is almost a closed psychological system.

Any attack on Trump can be used as evidence that Trump is right.

That is why economic failure alone is not enough

This is why I think it is a mistake to explain MAGA exclusively through economics.

Of course, economics matters.

Inflation matters.

Jobs matter.

Immigration matters.

Taxes matter.

Trade matters.

But political identity can survive material losses.

A person can pay more because of tariffs and still support Trump.

He can be dissatisfied with healthcare.

He can be dissatisfied with schools.

He can be dissatisfied with government services.

He can consider Trump a poor administrator.

But if at the same time he receives another form of reward — the feeling that those who spent years demonstrating their superiority over him are finally being humiliated — then the economic cost becomes part of an entirely different bargain.

The subject of that bargain is not money.

The subject is status.

And then the MAGA slogan takes on a somewhat different meaning.

Make America Great Again can psychologically become:

Make Me Great Again.

Not necessarily literally.

And not necessarily consciously.

But the mechanism is something like that.

Why Trump is particularly suited to this role

And here we return to the strangest paradox.

Trump is so far removed from his voter that this is precisely what makes him effective.

He does not have to be a farmer.

He does not have to be an electrician.

He does not have to be a worker.

He has to be someone capable of defeating those whom his supporters regard as their oppressors.

He does not need to be “one of them.”

He needs to be their weapon.

And this explains one of the strangest elements of MAGA:

The more obviously Trump belongs to the wealthy elite, the less necessarily this destroys his image as an anti-elite leader.

Because his function has changed.

He is no longer simply a man from the elite.

He becomes a man who uses his power against another elite.

And “the other elite” is gradually defined not by wealth.

And this is very important.

A professor may earn less than the owner of a construction company.

A scientist may be significantly poorer than a politician.

A teacher may be poorer than almost any entrepreneur.

A government expert may own no significant property at all.

But in the political imagination, they can remain “the elite” because they possess another resource:

The right to say what is true.

And this is the resource Trump attacks particularly consistently.

The main battle of MAGA is not about wealth

If we look at MAGA simply as a movement against the rich, numerous contradictions emerge.

Why then a billionaire?

Why Wall Street?

Why big business?

Why tax cuts for the wealthy?

Why inherited capital?

Why does a man who built a political career around his own wealth become the defender of “ordinary people”?

Because MAGA is not a classic war of the poor against the rich.

It is a war of status.

And in this war, a university professor may become a more convenient target than a billionaire.

Because the professor possesses symbolic capital.

He says:

“I know.”

Trump responds:

“You don't know anything.”

And that sentence is politically much more powerful than an argument about a tax rate.

That is why criticism of Trump often doesn't work

There is another uncomfortable fact.

People who want to defeat Trump by listing his lies often fail to understand what they are actually arguing against.

They think they are presenting evidence.

But for part of MAGA, the very sources of that evidence are already part of the problem.

CNN says Trump lied?

CNN is the enemy.

The New York Times says the economy is deteriorating?

The New York Times is the enemy.

Scientists say otherwise?

Scientists are bought.

A judge rules against Trump?

The judge is politicized.

A federal official criticizes the administration?

Deep state.

In other words, the argument ceases to be about facts.

It becomes an argument about who has the right to determine the facts.

And that is a much deeper conflict.

That is why Trump may turn out to be not the cause, but the prototype.

Trump may be a prototype rather than the cause

At this point, I would stop viewing Trump as a unique historical character.

Perhaps Trump is simply the first major political figure of an era in which the crisis of institutional authority has become an independent political resource.

He discovered that you can win elections without convincing people of your own competence.

You can win by convincing them that competence itself is not worthy of trust.

And if that is true, then after Trump we are not going to see the end of this phenomenon.

We are going to see it scale.

Trump may leave.

MAGA may change.

The Republican Party may change its leader.

But the question will remain:

What happens to a society in which every person has the ability to live inside their own personalized system of intellectual confirmation?

And here artificial intelligence enters the picture.

In the past, in order to create your own system of intellectual confirmation, you had to find the right television channel, blogger, journalist, YouTube channel or pseudo-expert.

Now you only need to formulate the right prompt.

You can receive a convincing explanation of why scientists are wrong.

Why economists do not understand economics.

Why judges are corrupt.

Why journalists lie.

Why institutions are working against you.

And the most dangerous thing here is not even that artificial intelligence can generate falsehoods.

Much more dangerous is this:

It can very convincingly rationalize beliefs that already exist.

If every person gets their own “expert” who confirms their particular view of the world, then the crisis of institutional authority ceases to be purely a political phenomenon.

It becomes technologically scalable.

The most uncomfortable part of the story

So perhaps the most uncomfortable part of Trumpism is not that it taught people to love Trump.

It taught them to question the very principle of authority.

Not to ask:

“What do the experts say, and how convincing is their evidence?”

But to ask:

“Who benefits from me believing them?”

The first question can lead to knowledge.

The second can be useful as a tool of critical thinking.

But if the second becomes the only question, society gradually loses the ability to distinguish between expert error and expertise itself.

And that is far more dangerous than any particular politician.

Because a president's term ends.

Institutions damaged by distrust take much longer to rebuild.

And perhaps that is why the main question of the future is no longer who will become the next Trump.

It is this:

Who will explain to a person why they are always right?
