Are All Migrant Workers the Same?
November 2026

Many years ago, I wanted to have my own farm and produce cheese and other dairy products. It was a long time ago, and it all happened in New York. Just one of the interesting episodes of my life.

In just one year, I went from one of Canada's best private schools, where Prince Andrew had once studied, to working for cash in New York as an immigrant. This was in the early 1990s. I wasn't even twenty yet.

Life then took me through all sorts of twists and turns. It was quite an experience. The year and a half I spent in New York as a migrant worker turned out to be eventful. I worked at a garment factory in Queens — not as a seamster, of course, but as a general laborer, so to speak. Then I got into moving, helping people relocate. Americans move house quite often. Many rent their homes, especially in big cities. During that time, I visited Barbara Streisand's house and many other interesting places.

And quite recently, by pure coincidence, life brought me back to the subject of dairy products. From two different directions, so to speak. One of them has to do with Petrovsky Farmers' Market. People from Rublyovka and Novaya Riga know the place. It has excellent steaks, mussels and sea urchins. Fruit and vegetables, too. Its tandoor oven is probably the best I've seen in the area. But today we're talking about dairy products. The selection is mixed — some good, some, frankly, not so good.

Some vendors simply mix cottage cheese bought at Chizhik, where a pack costs less than 100 rubles, and sell it for 2,000 rubles per kilogram.

I ask:

— How can they do that? Don't they understand?

I'm told:

— They're rich assholes. They don't care. Two thousand rubles is nothing to them.

— But why are they assholes? — I ask.

— They're Muscovites, that's why. Living the high life.

I don't mention that I was born here myself. So were my parents, and even some of my grandparents.

I speak to some other people. The owner is a believer, a bearded Orthodox Christian. The shop is named after Patriarch Alexy. I start looking into the details, and things turn out to be even worse than with those who mix cheap cottage cheese from Chizhik. Expiration dates are relabeled. The vendors are so full of hatred for the people they sell their goods to that I feel uncomfortable even thinking about buying cottage cheese, yogurt or sour cream from them. The products are displayed in little jars without factory seals or packaging that would normally make it impossible to open them without leaving a trace. Here, you can pick up a jar, open it, close it again and put it back on the shelf. Quality control? None whatsoever.

I say:

— But he's a man of God.

— What kind of man of God is he? — they reply. — He's a scoundrel and a crook. He simply married the adopted daughter of the owner of the entire Petrovsky Market.

— So why does he relabel the dates on cheese and other products?

— The rent! Do you know how much the rent is here? And everything is paid in cash. Or almost everything. It's a little kingdom of its own. Besides, what is there to take from Muscovites other than money? They don't want to do anything themselves.

Then my conversation partner turns to me and says:

— You're a Muscovite, I can hear it. You wouldn't understand.

And that's when I remembered my time in New York. These people talk about Muscovites in exactly the same way that Russian-speaking immigrants in New York used to talk about Americans many years ago: lazy, slow, and clumsy. If I had the opportunities they have, I'd get so much done! But they just sit around and can't even be bothered to get off their backsides.

I think to myself: how interesting. Are all migrant workers more or less the same?

I ask:

— So are the shop owners Muscovites, then?

— Not at all. See that Asian guy over there? I think he's Kyrgyz. He has two cafés and a tandoor here. He's already bought himself two apartments on Tverskaya Street.

— And what about that one? — I ask, pointing at the owner of the dairy shop.

— The holy man, you mean? — he asks me.

— That guy is a complete bastard. His staff turnover is higher than anyone else's. He cheats and rips off even his own employees.

— And he's not afraid? Don't people complain about him?

— I don't know. But he does a good job of pretending to be cleaner than everyone else. Not all his business is cash-only. He even has 1C accounting software installed. Apparently, he thinks that makes people lower their guard. We're the market! A market for the elite, that's all.

— I saw Lyubov Uspenskaya here today, — I say. — Though I wouldn't exactly call her part of the elite.

— Garik Kharlamov comes here too, — my conversation partner replies. — Even Gref used to drop by. Lots of wealthy people come here. They all have bodyguards. There are also hockey training facilities nearby. Parents often stop in to buy something while their children are at practice.

— And they actually buy Chizhik cottage cheese for 2,000 rubles?

— You bet they do. And they praise it, too.

I walked out and thought to myself: are all migrant workers the same? Is there a little territorial conqueror lurking inside every one of them, deep down — someone who sees the local population's territory as something to take over?
