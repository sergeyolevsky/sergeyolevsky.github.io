“Tiana”: How Syrian Capital in 1990s Moscow Was Built on Blood and Stolen Millions
September 2026

This story remained in the shadows for a long time — buried in the archives of the Moscow Criminal Investigation Department (MUR) and the Lyublino District Police Department. A significant portion of these materials was never digitized.

But for me, this is also a personal story.

My father was a scientist. He specialized in solid-state physics. In 1988, he moved into cooperative business. First, he was invited to join the Poisk cooperative. Then he opened his own cooperative, Iskatel.

The business grew rapidly. A whole group of companies emerged around Iskatel, and by 1993, approximately two thousand people worked across the group.

Now to how it all ended.

My father was the superior of Alla Drozd. Alla was his junior partner in several commercial projects. I personally knew people who would later find themselves at the center of this story.

Soslan Molnar, Alla’s son, studied with me at Marymount Manhattan College in New York. My father paid for both of our education.

When Soslan returned to Russia after his studies, I remained in the United States.

For me, the Tiana story is not a retelling of old newspaper articles. I remember Nabil Al-Ashkar. I know this story not only from archival materials.

This was a time when one could become a shareholder of a bank after contributing two Belarus tractors to its charter capital. Back then, the word “Imperial” meant nothing — it was simply a rather amusing name for a new bank.

This is a chronicle of how international business emerged at the intersection of Perestroika and the wild capitalism of the 1990s — and at what cost fortunes were built that would later move to the Middle East.

At the center of this story are the Tiana joint venture, my father, the Syrian businessman Nabil Al-Ashkar, several million dollars that disappeared as a result of a commercial dispute, and people for whom a disagreement over money gradually went far beyond ordinary business relations.

The KGB Connection and the Birth of Tiana

It was 1991.

It was during this period that my father’s acquaintance Alla Tolstikhina and his former scientific colleague, whose father was a serving KGB general named Smirnov, helped arrange a key introduction.

General Smirnov introduced the aspiring Russian businessman to a Syrian entrepreneur named Nabil Al-Ashkar. The documents also contain the spelling “Nabil Alashkar.”

Together, they decided to establish a joint venture.

Initially, the Syrian wanted to name the company “Diana,” after his wife. However, that name was already taken in Moscow.

My father then came up with a simple solution: he changed the first letter, and the unusual name Tiana appeared in the official records.

It later emerged that Nabil was related to a well-known Syrian security official named Gaidar — or, in the Arabic form, Haydar.

This was how the story began: a business that very quickly moved far beyond the framework of an ordinary Soviet cooperative and became connected with international trade, large foreign-currency transactions, and people who had very different ideas about the rules of doing business.

The $4 Million Fraud and the Betrayal by Sokayev

In 1992, my father transferred approximately $4.2–4.3 million to Nabil Al-Ashkar to purchase and deliver a large shipment of goods to Russia.

Instead of marketable goods, however, Nabil sent what was effectively an unsellable shipment.

My father formally returned the unusable goods and demanded the full return of the foreign currency.

The money was never returned.

After that, the commercial dispute gradually developed into a much more serious confrontation.

Nabil brought Timur Sokayev into the conflict.

Sokayev was a professional freestyle wrestler, a former European junior champion, and competed for Moscow’s Spartak.

He had previously worked at Tiana, but was dismissed after corruption schemes connected with him were uncovered. Earlier, he had also been associated with the Iskatel cooperative.

According to the information available to me, Sokayev was assisted by a man named Solomatin — my father’s and Nabil’s Arabic interpreter. He graduated from MGIMO and spoke Arabic and English. I believe Solomatin later pursued a career connected with the Ministry of Foreign Affairs. Today, there is an ambassador with that surname in one Arab country. I cannot make any claims, however, because I remember only his surname and last saw him in 1992.

In effect, Nabil brought Sokayev and people around him into the conflict to put pressure on his former business partner.

And from there, the conflict began to move beyond the boundaries of an ordinary commercial dispute.

Threats, violence and attempts on people’s lives followed.

The first victim was Malinin, head of the Department of Public Education of Moscow’s Eastern Administrative District.

I no longer remember his exact first name. Most likely Alexander, like the famous singer.

But I remember the man himself very well: an honored teacher, a wonderful, educated and highly erudite person. In appearance, he somewhat resembled Lenin.

He was attacked. He suffered more than ten stab wounds and died.

As far as I know, neither the perpetrator nor the person who ordered the killing was ever identified.

The confrontation with Nabil and Timur continued for approximately two years.

Eventually, the business effectively collapsed.

Nabil never returned to Russia and never returned the approximately $4.3 million.

As far as the history can be reconstructed today, this money became an important starting point for the subsequent fate of Nabil Al-Ashkar’s capital.

What Happened to Nabil

Decades later, I decided to trace Nabil’s subsequent history using modern open sources.

In Damascus, I found a commercial listing for “Muhammad Nabil Al-Ashkar and Sons” — محمد نبيل الأشقر و أولاده.

The listing gives an address in the historic commercial district of Al-Harika, as well as several landline telephone numbers, a mobile number and a fax number.

The name of the family business is almost identical to the one I was able to identify in modern sources.

There is, however, an important caveat.

Open sources cannot establish with absolute certainty that this contemporary Syrian commercial listing refers to the same Nabil Al-Ashkar whom I knew in Moscow in the early 1990s.

But there is another detail that makes the listing worth noting.

The name refers not simply to relatives, but specifically to “his sons.”

One of Nabil’s sons lived in the New York area. I personally spoke with him by telephone. I believe he became a doctor in the United States.

Nabil himself had an interest in medicine. At the time, another company he owned in Russia was involved in medicine and was called Avicenna.

This produces an intriguing picture.

The family story continued into the next generation — in Syria, the United States and, presumably, other jurisdictions.

And today, what interests me is not only the question of where Nabil went after leaving Moscow.

The more interesting question is what the family business became over the course of three decades — and what his children are doing today.

The Bloody Finale on Stavropolskaya Street

Four years later, on May 28, 1996, at approximately 2:00 p.m., a double murder took place in Moscow at 16/12 Stavropolskaya Street, inside the offices of Tiana Plus.

An armed man entered the director’s office and opened fire.

Alla Drozd and her adopted son Ibrahim were killed.

For some reason, the press incorrectly reported that Alla’s driver had been killed with her instead of Ibrahim. That was not the case. It was Ibrahim who was killed.

At the time of the attack, Alla was speaking on the telephone with a mutual acquaintance. The person on the other end of the line heard the screams and gunshots in real time.

Alla’s second son, Soslan Molnar, who carried his father’s surname, was downstairs at the time.

The attackers deliberately kept him downstairs while they went up to the office. Obviously, this was done to prevent him from raising an alarm and interfering with the attack.

That is why Soslan survived while his mother and brother did not.

Neither the organizer nor the direct perpetrator of the murders was identified.

On the night of May 29, 1996, officers from MUR and the Lyublino District Police Department carried out several arrests on Krasnodonskaya Street.

One of the main suspects was Timur Sokayev — a former Tiana employee and a man connected to Nabil Al-Ashkar.

Other people were also detained.

It is important, however, to distinguish between the fact of detention and proof of guilt: being detained does not in itself mean that a person committed a crime.

All those detained were subsequently released.

But the episode itself demonstrates how far the commercial conflict that had begun several years earlier had escalated by that point.

What Else Did Tiana Do?

Tiana was involved in far more than the business that is now associated with this story.

There was a trading house, a garment manufacturing operation, and a large cosmetics production business. The group also had interests in three banks.

One of them was Bank Elektronika, which I encountered later in my own professional banking career. Naturally, all of its shareholders and influential figures had changed by then.

Tiana was in the process of launching a large shampoo production facility, but the project was never completed.

Its Tiana hair gel, however, was very popular.

And here another interesting detail emerges.

Today, there is a company producing creams and other cosmetics under the name “Teana.”

Whether there is any connection to the original Tiana of the early 1990s, I do not know.

It would therefore be wrong to claim that there is.

But the coincidence of the name is interesting.

And What Happened to Bank Imperial?

Everyone remembers how that story ended.

Still, the people who passed through Imperial remain quite prominent and well known today.

With the possible exception of Rodionov, who decided to pursue a quieter business far from Russia.

But there is another part of the story that seems no less interesting today: where did those approximately 11 percent of Imperial that belonged personally to my father eventually go?

He entered Imperial as an individual shareholder.

The subsequent history of the bank led to LUKOIL gradually increasing its control over it.

But exactly who acquired my father’s stake, when, and for how much — that is a separate story.

And perhaps a very interesting one in its own right.

Epilogue

A criminal case concerning the double murder was opened in May 1996 in Moscow’s Lyublino District.

It is reasonable to assume that the case materials are still held within the archival system of the Investigative Committee of Russia.

They should contain witness testimony from people involved in the events, financial and commercial documents, statements, and information about the connections between the people who found themselves at the center of this story.

For me, this story begins with Nabil Al-Ashkar.

A Syrian businessman who arrived in Moscow during the Perestroika period established a commercial structure there and later left Russia following a conflict in which millions of dollars disappeared.

His family subsequently continued its commercial activities in Syria and other countries.

Today, the heirs to this story may appear to be perfectly respectable businessmen engaged in international commerce.

But the archives remember a different story.

The story of Tiana.

The story of $4.3 million.

The story of betrayal.

The story of a commercial dispute that gradually turned into violence.

And the story of blood on Stavropolskaya Street.

Sometimes this is what the real history of the origins of capital looks like — when you look not at what its owners eventually became, but at where it all began.
