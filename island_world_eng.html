When an Island Becomes the Center of the World
October 2026

Recently, I saw a LinkedIn post by Steve Berges accompanied by a photograph of a child wearing expensive clothes and holding an expensive smartphone, along with a reflection on how capitalism had disappointed him. In the comments, Paul Lobosco — a financial consultant, president of Forward Capital Management and founder of American Health Revolution — wrote: “We are a spiritually sick nation.” I replied: “When do you think it happened — and why?” He explained that LinkedIn was not the place for such a long discussion and then outlined his position: in his view, the situation remained manageable until the United States abandoned the gold standard and became the world's reserve currency, after which came many trillions of dollars of monetary creation and a political system that placed corporate profits above the interests of people.

All names and quotations used in this article come from a public discussion on LinkedIn. They refer exclusively to statements that the individuals themselves posted publicly. The names are mentioned not to judge the people or their professional activities, but because their public exchange was the starting point for this article.

Lobosco's explanation could itself be examined at considerable length, because in one short comment he effectively combined several very different historical processes — the abandonment of gold convertibility, the subsequent development of the fiat monetary system, the massive expansion of the money supply, changes in the structure of the American economy and political system, and the feeling that economic growth increasingly serves corporations and owners of capital. At the same time, his statement that the United States became the world's reserve currency after abandoning the gold standard is not entirely accurate historically: the dollar had already occupied a central position in the international monetary system long before 1971. But what interests me here is not even how convincing Lobosco's explanation is from the perspective of monetary history. What interests me is a much broader question: why does a person trying to explain what is happening in his own country choose precisely this particular framework and regard these particular causes as the most important ones?

And this, I think, leads to a much more interesting subject — the subject of an island gradually becoming the center of the world.

If you look at a map, the United States is indeed in a rather unusual geographical position: the Atlantic Ocean lies between it and Europe, the Pacific Ocean between it and Asia, while its direct land connections to the outside world are limited to two neighbors, one of which is itself an enormous country. Yet over the past century or more, the United States has become one of the world's principal economic, financial, technological, cultural and political centers — so large that its internal reality has itself become comparable in complexity and diversity to what a person might once have imagined only as the “outside world.”

There is an important qualification here: this does not mean that the United States has become some economically self-sufficient fortress that no longer needs the outside world. Almost the opposite is true. The American economy is deeply integrated into international trade, global supply chains, international capital flows, imports and exports, while American companies, universities, financial institutions and technology platforms have long been part of a global system that extends physically and economically far beyond North America. The paradox lies elsewhere: a country can be extremely closely connected to the rest of the world and at the same time possess such a huge domestic market, such a developed internal infrastructure of knowledge, capital, technology and culture, and such a strong attraction for people from around the world that someone living inside this system does not need to constantly look outward to experience the scale of the world around him.

In this sense, globalization can produce a rather unexpected consequence: the more of the world comes to you, the less you need to go looking for it.

When one country contains the world's major financial centers, leading universities, technology companies of global scale, a huge consumer market, a highly developed entertainment industry, international corporations, the largest media platforms and enormous numbers of people who have arrived from different parts of the planet, the outside world does not disappear. But a significant part of its economic, intellectual and cultural energy becomes directly embedded in everyday life. At some point, a person may stop perceiving all of this as something international, because the international has simply become ordinary.

This is where, in my view, an “island” system of coordinates begins to appear.

I do not mean isolationism, provincialism or ignorance of other countries. A person can have an excellent understanding of the outside world, travel, read international news and work with foreigners, and still remain inside a system of coordinates in which his own country, his own city, his own professional environment and his own social circle become the main reference point for judging reality. The island does not appear when a person knows nothing about the outside world. It appears when his internal reality becomes so large, rich and subjectively self-sufficient that it begins to feel like all the world he needs.

After all, people almost never compare themselves with humanity as a whole. They compare themselves with the people around them — neighbors, colleagues, classmates, people in their professional circles and those they see online. As a result, a standard of living that might look extremely high by global standards can quickly become ordinary within a particular social environment and eventually cease to feel remarkable at all.

If everyone around you drives good cars, a good car stops being a sign of high income. If people in your circle travel abroad several times a year, an international trip stops being exceptional. If an expensive smartphone is practically universal, it no longer feels like a luxury item. If a certain level of income is normal within your professional environment, you begin to perceive it as the baseline, even if by global standards that income is far above average.

I remember very clearly how differently this system worked in the Soviet Union.

A person who had a VCR at home was considered relatively well-off, because the very fact of owning a VCR told people more about the family's material circumstances than owning an expensive smartphone does today. A good carpet hanging on the wall could also be a visible symbol of prosperity: carpets were expensive, difficult to obtain and occupied roughly the same place in the Soviet idea of a prosperous apartment that completely different objects might occupy today.

Several decades passed, and something almost absurd happened: the very object that could once have testified to a family's prosperity can now, if it has survived at all, easily end up at a country house in front of a garage or lying somewhere around the pets, while the VCR has become practically a museum exhibit. But the interesting point is not the fate of the carpet or the VCR themselves. It is that the objects have hardly changed — what changed is the place they occupy in our system of coordinates.

Once, a VCR was a luxury. Then it became ordinary household equipment. Then it disappeared from everyday life altogether. A carpet was once a sign of prosperity, and later became so common and so outdated that its former symbolic value almost completely disappeared. What changed was not so much the object as our idea of what significance that object should have.

The same thing happens with living standards as a whole.

A society in which a certain level of material well-being becomes widespread gradually stops perceiving that level as high. It simply becomes normal. And when the normal level rises, the threshold of dissatisfaction rises with it: a person no longer compares himself with those who live significantly worse, but with those who are slightly better off. As a result, an objective increase in opportunities does not necessarily produce a subjective feeling of greater well-being.

This is why a perfectly well-off person can sincerely feel that he is constantly missing something, even though if his lifestyle were placed in a historical or global context, it would be much closer to the traditional idea of wealth than to poverty. His feeling does not necessarily have to be false. He is simply comparing himself not with humanity, and not even with the previous generation, but with the island on which he lives.

And the larger that island becomes, the higher its internal standard becomes.

In this sense, the United States represents a particularly interesting example, because American domestic reality has become so vast over several generations that a person living inside it often has no practical need to compare it with the outside world every day. The world is already there — in the shops, universities, companies, technologies, internet, films, music, finance, restaurants, airports, professional environments and people who have arrived from every continent.

This is why I would not explain American changes exclusively through the abandonment of the gold standard, monetary creation or even financialization of the economy, although all of these factors certainly matter. They can help explain the distribution of wealth, changes in the value of money, asset-price growth, structural changes in the economy and many other processes. But they are much less capable of explaining a change in the very human scale of normality — the moment when a certain level of consumption and opportunity stops feeling exceptional and becomes the minimum acceptable standard.

And that is no longer just economics.

It is the psychology of a society.

This is why it seems insufficient to simply say that modern people have become more materialistic or that capitalism has made people unhappy. Something more complicated may be happening: a person finds himself inside an environment so rich and diverse that its possibilities begin to feel natural and practically unlimited. He then becomes disappointed not necessarily because he objectively has too little, but because his own system of coordinates has grown even faster than his actual possibilities.

At the same time, a country can remain deeply globalized while the individual living inside it can be completely non-global in his perception of the world.

A company may sell products on five continents, its employees may work with people from dozens of countries, money may move between countries in seconds, and technology may connect billions of users. Yet a particular person can still perceive his own economic and cultural environment as the natural background of life, while everything else feels external, additional and, in some sense, secondary.

This is not necessarily good or bad.

It is simply a property of a very large center.

And here the main paradox appears.

The more strongly a country becomes a center of the world economy and culture, the less its inhabitants may need to consciously recognize that they are living at the center. The center stops feeling like the center because, for the person living inside it, the center is simply the surrounding reality.

Perhaps this is why the strangest form of isolation is not a situation in which you have been cut off from the world. A much more unusual situation occurs when the world becomes so deeply connected to you that the need to constantly look at it from the outside gradually disappears.

The more central an island becomes, the less it feels like an island.

And perhaps this is one of the most interesting characteristics of modern America: not that it is detached from the world, but that so much of the world has already been incorporated into its own reality that the outer boundary of that reality becomes almost invisible.

You can understand this only by stepping outside your own island for a moment — not to declare it right or wrong, not to condemn the people who live on it, and not even to prove that somewhere there is another and more correct model of life, but simply to see the ocean again and remember how enormous the world is beyond the system of coordinates we have come to regard as natural.
