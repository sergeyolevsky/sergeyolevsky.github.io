Who Makes Decisions and Why Opportunities Go Unrealized
October 2026

I always assumed that people were capable of reaching obvious conclusions on their own. That there was no need to spell out every thought, drop hints, or explain several times something that, to me, seemed perfectly clear.

But the longer I spent in the corporate world, the more obvious the opposite became. People do not always arrive at the right conclusion on their own. And even when they have heard your argument and seem to agree with it, that is not enough. You have to repeat your arguments, defend your position several times, and then, quite frankly, start persuading people to do what logic already tells them to do.

I have always been irritated by excessive caution and bureaucracy. Some people find them necessary; others, like me, find them frustrating. But that is not even the main issue. The problem begins when procedure becomes more important than results, and discussing an opportunity takes the place of actually trying to realize it.

African Resources and the Price of Political Illusions

How many times does one have to explain that Africa's mineral resources can be more than just a source of raw materials? They can also be a means of generating foreign-currency revenue.

You can talk about this endlessly, especially if you are talking to people who are not actually in a position to make a decision.

Even then, you may hear something like this: "Yes, that's all true. But our current priority is the Sahel countries that have broken with France and are now our allies."

Why those countries in particular? Who decided that a political realignment automatically makes a country a reliable long-term partner? Do the people making these decisions take the region's history into account, and how quickly the political situation there can change?

I have witnessed such changes firsthand.

In Nigeria, changes of government took place through relatively democratic processes, while the system itself continued to function. Leaders changed, but state institutions, business relationships, and the ability to continue existing projects remained.

In Niger, things unfolded differently.

We had warm personal meetings with the country's leader at the time, and there were agreements in place, including arrangements facilitated by our longtime friend, Air Marshal Ibrahim Alfa.

Then the president was overthrown.

I remember how quickly everything changed. The new authorities arrived at the presidential palace in jeeps and armored vehicles, and in an instant, the practical value of the previous agreements disappeared.

One day, you are meeting a president in Abuja, discussing prospects for cooperation and relying on the commitments you have secured. Then, just a couple of weeks later, the government changes in a neighboring country, and yesterday's political guarantees no longer guarantee anything.

That is why I consider it extremely short-sighted to assume that political alignment alone will allow you to establish a lasting presence in gold, uranium, and other mineral extraction projects in Niger, Mali, and other countries led by governments friendly to Russia.

The political loyalty of a particular leader is not the same thing as a stable investment environment. Nor is it a substitute for working properly with state institutions, licensing authorities, local partners, and the economics of a project.

But that is my point of view. People in senior offices may think differently.

It Is Not Enough to Know What Needs to Be Done. You Must Know Who Makes the Decisions.

Of course, it is essential to communicate with people who make decisions. The problem is that sometimes it is not entirely clear who those people actually are.

I remember speaking with Iskander Khalilov, the head of LUKOIL Overseas. Essentially, he was exactly the person I needed to speak to in order to develop projects in Nigeria.

But by that time, he was already leaving LUKOIL to pursue his own ventures. The people who, as I recall, succeeded him and were associated with Perm proved practically impossible to reach.

As a result, you can know exactly whom you need to speak to, see a concrete opportunity, and have the necessary contacts on the ground, yet still fail to reach the person who can turn all of that into a decision.

Recently, I met with the management of a subsidiary of one of Russia's best-known industrial holdings. They are interested in operating precisely in those countries from which, as the popular expression goes, "the French have been kicked out."

Incidentally, I can no longer remember whether I told them the story of how I witnessed the change of power in Niger. How, not long before, we had held personal meetings and reached agreements, only for the political situation to change within a matter of weeks.

Perhaps I did not tell them. But that is not really the point.

My impression was that the meeting itself was needed primarily to demonstrate that an instruction had been carried out. I had initially written to the chairman of the holding company, a person well known in the country. In response, I was referred to a meeting with the management of one of its subsidiaries.

From the perspective of corporate procedure, everything looked perfectly logical. The letter was reviewed, the relevant executive was identified, and a meeting was held.

But does that mean anyone is genuinely interested in making the project happen? I am not sure.

You can hold dozens of meetings, prepare presentations, exchange business cards, and end up exactly where you started.

A Local King Is Not the State

There is another common mistake, particularly among those who are just beginning to work in Africa's mining industry.

Suppose the subject is gold. People reach an agreement with a local king and assume that all the important issues have now been resolved.

That is not how it works.

A traditional ruler may wield considerable influence in his territory. He can help establish access to the local community, resolve conflicts, facilitate relations with landowners, and address other local issues.

This is a valuable resource. Sometimes it is virtually impossible to get started without it.

However, a local king does not replace the federal government. He cannot issue all the necessary mining licences on behalf of the state, unilaterally determine export rules, or control the entire gold trade.

In Nigeria, you have to take into account the federal level, the relevant government agencies, statutory requirements, and the actual licensing mechanisms.

An agreement at the local level can be the starting point for a project, but it is not a legal or economic guarantee that the project will succeed.

That is why experience in a country matters, but not simply because you have spent time there. What matters is understanding how the system works, who actually has influence over what, and where one person's authority ends and another's begins.

Manganese: The Opportunity Is There, but Funding Is Needed

I now have another interesting project in Nigeria: a substantial manganese deposit.

The indicative volume of purchases from local suppliers could reach 50,000 tonnes per month. However, putting the project into operation would require substantial financing.

To understand the scale of the challenge, consider logistics alone.

According to preliminary estimates, transporting a single 30-tonne truckload costs 4 million naira.

Moving 50,000 tonnes would require approximately 1,667 truckloads. That comes to around 6.67 billion naira, or roughly US$5 million at the exchange rate used for this calculation.

Transportation alone. That figure excludes the cost of the raw material itself and all other expenses.

This is where talk about Africa's mineral wealth ends and the real economics of a project begin.

It is not enough to identify a deposit or find suppliers. You have to organize procurement, secure financing, build a logistics operation, verify the quality of the material, agree terms with a buyer, and calculate the entire chain from the point of purchase to the final sale.

Yet the opportunities can be substantial. If you have access to the material, a clear economic model, and a reliable buyer, you can build a sustainable trading operation and then scale it up.

And this brings us back to where we started.

An opportunity may be obvious. Its economic rationale may be easy to explain. The necessary contacts may have existed for years. But none of that is worth much until you find someone willing to make a decision, provide the financing, and take responsibility for the result.

Buy the raw material in rubles. Sell the manganese for foreign currency.

Of course, the purchase, transportation, and entire operating chain must be financed first. This is neither a simple arrangement nor money out of thin air.

But if we take a strategic view, why not consider Africa's mineral resources as one of the instruments for generating foreign-currency revenue, rather than merely as raw-material assets?

The opportunities are there. The question is who is prepared to realize them.

Everyone can draw their own conclusions.
