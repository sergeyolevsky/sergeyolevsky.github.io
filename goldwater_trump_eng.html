From Goldwater to Trump: How the Old Republican Party Disappeared
August 2026

In August 1974, something happened that seems almost unimaginable today.

After the release of the recording that became known as the “smoking gun,” it became clear that President Richard Nixon had been directly involved in an attempt to conceal the circumstances of the Watergate scandal. Three influential Republicans came to the White House — Senator Barry Goldwater, Senate Republican Leader Hugh Scott, and House Republican Leader John Rhodes.

They did not come to demand that Democrats stop the investigation. They did not argue that all the accusations were a political attack. They came to tell their own president that they could no longer support him.

Two days later, Nixon announced his resignation.

What is especially important is who told him this. Barry Goldwater was not a liberal Republican and not a political opponent of Nixon. He was one of the founders of modern American conservatism. But for him, there was a line between loyalty to the president and loyalty to the Constitution.

That line today seems almost to have disappeared.

But it would be a mistake to say that Donald Trump simply destroyed the old Republican Party. In reality, Trump was the final stage of a process that had been unfolding for several decades.

From Goldwater to Nixon

In 1964, Barry Goldwater lost to Lyndon Johnson by a huge margin. Yet his defeat was also the beginning of a long-term transformation of American conservatism.

Goldwater demonstrated that there was enormous demand in the country for a tougher conservative policy. Gradually, conservative voters in the South who had traditionally voted Democratic began moving into the Republican Party. This process accelerated particularly after the passage of civil rights legislation.

Richard Nixon managed to turn this trend into an effective electoral strategy.

But the Republican Party of that era was still a classic political coalition. It contained moderates and conservatives, business representatives and religious activists, internationalists and isolationists.

And most importantly — the party was not yet completely subordinated to one person.

That is why in 1974 the party leadership could come to the president and say: if you broke the law, we will no longer protect you.

The party must survive the president.

At the time, this idea seemed completely natural.

Reagan and the Transformation of Conservatism into a Mass Movement

In the 1980s, Ronald Reagan changed the Republican Party even further.

He brought together economic conservatives, anti-communists, religious conservatives, and an increasingly significant part of the white South.

Reagan, however, remained a politician of the old type. He was a committed conservative, but at the same time an optimist, a supporter of American institutions and American leadership.

Yet under Reagan, a fundamental shift took place.

The Republican Party became dependent not only on the party apparatus and business. It became a mass ideological movement.

And when politics becomes a movement, a danger emerges: people begin voting not simply for specific economic programs, but for a sense of belonging to a particular America.

A division emerges between “us” and “them.”

As long as this division remains under the control of the party leadership, the system still works.

But gradually, that control begins to disappear.

Gingrich: Politics Becomes War. The Gingrich Revolution.

One of the most important turning points was the emergence of Newt Gingrich.

In the 1990s, he introduced a fundamentally more aggressive model of political confrontation.

Democrats ceased to be merely political competitors. Increasingly, they were presented as a force threatening the country itself.

This was not simply a change in rhetoric. It was a change in political technology.

Conflict became a method of mobilizing voters.

If previously a Republican might say, “Democrats are proposing the wrong economic policy,” the new rhetoric increasingly sounded like: “Democrats are destroying America.”

In 1994, Republicans gained control of the House of Representatives.

From that point on, American politics gradually began turning into a permanent war between two parties.

Compromise became suspicious.

Giving ground to the opponent began to be perceived as a betrayal of one's own voters.

This is an extremely important moment for understanding the future of Trump.

Tea Party: The Revolt Against Washington

The next turning point came after the election of Barack Obama.

The Tea Party movement emerged.

Initially, this movement was very different from what MAGA would later become. Its main slogans were limiting the size of government, cutting taxes, reducing the national debt, and defending the Constitution.

In a sense, the Tea Party was a revolt against its own party establishment.

People trusted Washington less and less.

They believed that the political class lived in a separate reality, made decisions for ordinary Americans, then lost elections, returned to Washington, and continued doing the same thing.

This is where one of the most important psychological prerequisites for Trump emerged:

If traditional politicians do not keep their promises, perhaps what is needed is not a traditional politician.

The Tea Party initially wanted to limit government power.

But gradually a much more radical idea emerged: if the system does not work, it should not be reformed — it should be broken.

Trump: The Revolt Against His Own Party Establishment

In 2016, Donald Trump did something that had seemed impossible not long before.

He defeated his own party establishment.

Bush, McCain, Romney and others represented different versions of the traditional Republican Party. Trump represented something completely different.

His main political proposition was not a new economic theory.

His proposition was much simpler:

“I will destroy the people who piss you off.”

It proved incredibly effective.

For a significant part of the Republican electorate, the problem was no longer only taxes, immigration, or foreign policy.

The problem had become the political class itself.

Trump offered himself as a weapon against that class.

And this is where his historical role lies.

He did not simply become the leader of the Republican Party.

He changed the relationship between the party and its leader.

From Party to Movement

The old Republican Party operated on the assumption that there was a party, and the president was its representative.

Under Trump, the opposite gradually happened.

A movement emerged around a specific leader, and only afterward did the party adapt itself to that movement.

This is a fundamental change.

In a traditional party system, the president had to take the party into account.

In a personalist system, the party has to take the leader into account.

This is precisely why the history of Nixon and contemporary America creates such a powerful contrast.

In 1974, Republican leaders told the president:

“We can no longer support you.”

Today, part of the Republican Party reasons quite differently:

“If we stop supporting our president, the Democrats will take power.”

In other words, the question is no longer necessarily:

“Is the president right?”

It increasingly becomes:

“What will happen to us if we stop supporting him?”

This is a fundamentally different political psychology.

The Great Irony

This is where a historical paradox emerges.

Classical American conservatism has always viewed the concentration of government power with deep suspicion.

Reagan used to say that government is not the solution to the problem — government is the problem.

The Tea Party emerged partly out of fear of excessive expansion of government power.

But the modern MAGA movement has, to a significant extent, arrived at a completely different position: if the president is “our” president, he should be given enough power to defeat those considered enemies of the movement.

In other words, there has been an almost complete reversal.

From distrust of power — to trust in the power of a particular leader.

From protecting institutions — to protecting one's leader within those institutions.

From the idea that “the party must survive the president” — to the idea that “the party must win together with the president.”

What Actually Disappeared

Therefore, I would not say that the Republican Party itself has disappeared.

What is disappearing is a different type of Republican.

It is a person who is capable of being a conservative and at the same time saying

In 1974, party loyalty had a boundary. That boundary ran along the Constitution and institutions.

Today, partisan polarization is significantly stronger. For many politicians, the cost of breaking with their own president may be higher than the cost of supporting him.

That is precisely why that August evening in 1974 seems almost unimaginable today.

Three leaders of the president's own party came to him and told him that the party would not cover for him.

Not because they had stopped being Republicans.

But precisely because they considered themselves Republicans.

And perhaps this is the central historical paradox of the modern Republican Party.

It has become far more conservative, far more aggressive, and far more mobilized — but at the same time significantly less capable of telling its own leader: “No.”

From Goldwater through Nixon, Reagan, Gingrich and the Tea Party to Trump, there was not simply a succession of leaders.

The very logic of the party changed:

party → leader → movement

gradually became

leader → movement → party.

And this is perhaps the most important transformation in the history of modern American politics.
